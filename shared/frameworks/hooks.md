## 1. Why the hook carries the video

A hook is the first 2 to 6 seconds. In that window the viewer is rapidly absorbing visuals, text, and audio and deciding whether to opt in.

**Funnel math.** The second a viewer churns, nothing after that point matters. A 55-second video where they churn at 30 seconds means the last 25 seconds might as well have been a black screen. Churn at 4 seconds and 51 seconds were wasted. The hook matters exponentially more than the rest of the video.

**The bridge to trust.** The goal of content is building 1:1 trust at scale. The hook is the bridge that gets a viewer far enough in for trust to start transferring. No crossing, no trust.

**Hook = headline + intro in one.** Short-form has no headline, subheader, thumbnail, or title. Every other format gives a teaser that helps you opt in. In short-form, all of that opt-in pressure sits on the hook.

**Choice vs reflex.** With YouTube, blogs, email, TV, the viewer chooses to start based on a headline. Short-form is fed automatically, so the viewer reacts by reflex. More people start watching who never would have opted in. Your hook has to be that much stronger to keep them.

Spend 30 to 40% of total effort on the video improving the hook.

**The job of a hook is one thing: get the viewer to opt in.** It does that by opening an on-target curiosity loop *related to the value the video actually delivers*. On-target means this is clearly for me. A curiosity loop means there is an unanswered question here and information coming that will let me keep guessing at it. The "related to the value delivered" part is what stops a hook from being clickbait: a loop that the body never feeds is a rug pull.

Both halves run on contrast, the distance between what the viewer already believes and what you are about to tell them. `psychology.md` sections 2 and 3 explain why; the operational version is section 3 below.

## 2. The four components and alignment

Most people hear "hook" and think only of the spoken line. That is one of four components.

**In order of importance:**
1. **Text hook** (what you write on screen)
2. **Visual hook** (what you show: b-roll, graphics, or you on a-roll)
3. **Spoken hook** (what you say)
4. **Audio hook** (non-spoken audio: music and SFX)

People read and absorb visually faster than they hear. Visual and text are consumed first and matter more. This is why silent videos can still perform, while strong spoken words with weak visuals rarely do.

Attention flows: visual/text → spoken → visual/text.

Never write only the spoken line. A hook handed over without its text and visual is not a finished hook.

### Max alignment is the whole game

The difference between 500 and 500,000 views is alignment across all four components.

Say one thing verbally while the visual shows something slightly different and the hook loses power through comprehension loss. Comprehension is the degree to which the viewer actually understands what they are seeing and hearing. Misalignment breeds confusion, confusion kills opt-in.

**Good alignment (14M views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/C_iVgDcJLd1/
- Spoken: "These are called Lifesize floor plans. You can literally walk through your exact home design before you build it."
- Visual: clips of people walking through floor plans of their home
- Text: "Future of Home Design" / "Lifesize Floor Plans"
- Audio: chill music
- Why it works: text and visuals perfectly complement the spoken line and deepen the curiosity rabbit hole.

**Bad alignment (40K views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/DEnDBAuvxA7/
- Spoken: "This company is sending shipping containers into space. But here's the crazy part, they're not using rockets. They're building a space cannon to shoot them into orbit."
- Visual: space shuttles in space, then a homemade PVC pipe cannon
- Text: "Space Cannons Are Awesome"
- Audio: chill music, subtle SFX
- Why it failed: spoken said shipping containers, visuals showed rockets. Text said space cannons, visual showed a makeshift pipe prototype that was hard to imagine launching a container.

**Takeaway.** Think about hooks cohesively from the start. While writing the spoken hook, know what the text and visual hooks will be. If you do not, the edit will end up misaligned.

## 3. Order of operations

**Step 0. Audit the visuals already available.**
Existing footage, sourced b-roll, or an old clip may be worth building the hook around. For the sleep headband video (https://www.instagram.com/reel/DGyDvJ7PHbA/), clips of the headband and people sleeping with it already existed, which made "This headband..." the natural opening. If there is nothing good yet, no problem, the visual gets created later. This step is a gut check.

**Step 1. Establish the contrast before writing a line.**
Write down two things side by side: what this audience currently believes about the topic, and what you are going to tell them instead. If those two are not meaningfully different, stop. No amount of rewording fixes a hook with no contrast underneath it. Say so plainly and help find a real angle instead.

The take can come from either of two places: genuinely new information, or a different angle on information the audience already has. The second is the one people overlook, and it is much more available than the first.

**Step 2. Write the spoken hook.**

*Easy mode:* pick a validated structure from section 4, ideally one already proven in the niche, and copy it.

*Normal mode:* write from scratch by answering two questions.
1. What context do I need to communicate so the viewer can decide to opt in?
2. What is the contrast between what they currently believe and what I believe?

Can both fit in one sentence? If not, use two. Write 1 to 4 lines.

Fewer words is better, but add words back if they prevent misunderstanding. Clarity beats brevity every time.

**Step 3. Identify the best visual to complement it.**

*Easy mode:* copy a validated visual hook structure from section 4.

*Normal mode:* if there is a clear visual that adds comprehension, use it. If not, three options:
- Shoot full-screen b-roll on a phone reflecting the action being described
- Generate full-screen graphics with AI tools
- Use full-screen creator a-roll (this puts more weight on the text hook)

**Step 4. Write the text hook.**

*Easy mode:* copy a validated text hook structure from section 4.

*Normal mode:* if the visual is only creator a-roll, the text hook has to be dialled. It should elicit curiosity and ideally an emotional reaction, shock or surprise, in the fewest words possible. This gets added in the edit.

Easy mode exists for all three written components, not just the spoken line. Copying a structure already proven in the niche is the highest-percentage move available at every step.

**Step 5. Hand off to production.**
Shooting, on-screen type treatment, cutting pace and the final playback test are covered in `production-notes.md`. When delivering a hook, include the notes that affect how it gets shot and cut, since alignment established in the writing can still be lost in the edit.

## 4. The structures library

Treat each section as a box of Lego. Take one piece from each and stack until the combination works.

Every option in each category is ordered easiest (top) to hardest (bottom) to execute. Find the easiest path that works and matches the desired brand aesthetic.

The fastest route to a winning formula is copying a proven style from someone in the same niche. See `hook-research.md` for how to find those.

### Spoken hook structures

**Fortuneteller** — curiosity loop comparing the present to a new future because of something that happened.
> "This backpack is going to change how people hike in the future"

**Experimentation** — curiosity loop framing a practical pain point being solved through an example or experiment shown live.
> "You can pack more hiking equipment in your backpack, if you just do this..."

**Educational / Tutorial** — curiosity loop framing a pain point solved through a method or tool being taught.
> "If you want to fit more in your backpack, use the 2-1-3 method"

**Secret Reveal** — curiosity loop by revealing a secret, hidden truth, or finding.
> "There's a backpack brand nobody knows about but all the celebrities are wearing"

**Contrarian / Negative** — curiosity loop by stating a non-obvious belief in the first line.
> "People spend way too much time obsessing over their backpack design"

**Comparison** — curiosity loop by comparing multiple versions of something.
> "These are the top 5 hiking backpack brands, but which one is actually best for long trips?"

**Question** — curiosity loop by posing a question they want answered.
> "Why are so many people wearing this backpack when they go hiking?"

**Raw Shock** — can be combined with any of the above. An instant scroll-stop word, action, or sound, or explicitly telling them to look at something.
> "See this bag..."

*Delivery:*
- **Synced with visual** — audio recorded live by the person on screen
- **Unsynced with visual** — audio recorded after, visuals laid on top

### Visual hook structures

*Visual selection:*
- Creator a-roll — full screen shot of creator talking to camera
- Creator in b-roll — full screen of creator doing something silently (audio usually recorded separately)
- Subject in b-roll — full screen of someone else doing something
- Non-subject b-roll — full screen of something that is not a person
- Examples — graphics that are visual examples (other videos, photos)
- Image screenshots — still images
- Article screenshot — screenshots of articles
- Arrow / highlights — arrows, circles, or highlights layered on to direct attention

*Visual movement, a-roll (talking):*
- None
- Slow zoom
- Quick zoom
- Crazy transition

*Visual movement, b-roll (non-talking):*
- None
- Quick cuts
- Swipe transitions
- Text overlays
- Slow zooms
- 3D effect

*Layout structures:*
- Full screen a-roll (in studio)
- Full screen a-roll (in world)
- Full screen a-roll with partial graphic overlay
- Full screen b-roll
- Full screen b-roll with a-roll overlaid (greenscreen or circle)
- Split screen a-roll + b-roll

### Text hook structures

*Word structures:*
- Base captions (repeat of spoken words)
- [X subject] does [Y thing]
- Future of [X thing]
- [X shocking thing]

*Visual layout:*
- Plain text
- Text with shadow
- Text on colored background
- Text written on a physical prop

*Motion:*
- Text appears
- Text swipes in from the side

### Audio hook structures
- No audio (zero music, zero SFX)
- Music only
- SFX only
- Both music and SFX

This is a library of options to experiment with, not a checklist to complete. Pull the pieces that fit the topic and the format the creator can actually execute.

## 5. The hook checklist

Run this before presenting anything.

1. **All four components present.** Spoken, text, visual, audio. Not just the line.
2. **Contrast is real.** The common belief and the contrarian reality are written down and genuinely different.
3. **On-target in the first sentence.** Someone hearing only line one knows whether this video is for them.
4. **No wasted real estate.** No "in my opinion", "what if I told you", "guys you won't believe this" burning the most valuable seconds.
5. **"You" not "me".** Unless the premise is so relatable that "I" reads as "everyone".
6. **One reading only.** If the opening could be read two ways, rewrite it. Ambiguity kills reflexive opt-in.
7. **The components agree.** Spoken, text and visual are all pointing at the same idea.
8. **Variants, not a single line.** Give 3 to 4 options built on different angles. Hooks are tested, not chosen.

## 6. Common questions

**Is there a "too salesy" line?**
The only goal is getting the viewer to keep going. If you sell and they see no incoming value, they bounce. If you push hard but framed so predicted value exceeds the push, they stay. It is about how much expected value is coming, not how hard you sell.

**Does Instagram differ from TikTok or YouTube Shorts?**
Good storytelling is good storytelling. Viewers cannot outrun their own psychology on any platform, so the best hooks work everywhere without change. That said, remixing formats already proven on a specific platform is a strong strategy, and optimal visual approaches do differ, so customize the visuals accordingly.

**Mindset or self-help hooks that are not cheesy?**
This always works: "If you want to improve [X problem], do [Y thing]." Self-help is not what is cheesy. Cheesiness comes from the delivery of the value trigger. Make the promise compelling and not too good to be true and there is no cheesiness problem.

**Reaction video hooks?**
Reaction videos surf on top of already-validated videos, so the hooks are pre-optimized. Strong format, works in every niche. The downside is that trust accrued to the reacting creator is stunted, since value-per-second coming from them is lower. Often worth it if the views are 100x.

**How do I turn dry facts and stats into hooks?**
Lead with the pain point they are experiencing, or the reward state they want, then use the stat to amplify why listening matters. Stats are not dry when they prove the incoming solution is more valuable than the viewer assumed.

**How do I gut check a hook that looks good to me?**
- Have a friend in the target audience watch and gauge their reaction
- Make 4 hook variants and A/B/C/D test them (Instagram Trial Reels works for this)
- Unless you copy a validated hook exactly, you cannot know for certain. Be willing to post, and if the hook fails, remake the video with a new hook a few weeks later.

**What data shows whether a hook is working?**
Average watch time. Above-average watch time means the hook was more effective than usual, and it will coincide with more views and better scores across every meaningful metric.

**What hooks work for physical products?**
Show the outcome, or the emotion from the outcome, that the subject gets from using the product.

## Fitness
Channel: https://www.instagram.com/jeremyethier/reels/

**Good (12M views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAjEew1PKBY/
- Spoken: "Why do some people squat like this, and others like this. Which form is correct?"
- Text: captions matching spoken
- Visual: A vs B squatting, with squat angle illustrated
- Why it worked: immediate context that this is about squat technique, with a perfect visual match. The curiosity loop compares two forms, and anyone who has squatted compares both to themselves.

**Bad (90K views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/CjlG3rEDP1Y/
- Spoken: "In my opinion, for natural lifters, a body fat of 12-15% is the most sustainable physique"
- Text: captions matching spoken
- Visual: creator deadlifting, shifts to a shirtless photo
- Why it failed: "In my opinion" wastes the most valuable real estate. Context was clear (body fat) but the takeaway was not. No comparison point, so no contrast and no curiosity loop.
- Better: "Bodybuilders make it seem like 6% bodyfat is normal. But if you want to look fit year round, 12-15% body fat is the best target. Here's how to get there in 6 weeks."

## Marketing agency
Channel: https://www.instagram.com/brian_blum/

**Good (320K views):** https://www.instagram.com/brian_blum/reel/DG10oXktDvJ/
- Spoken: "Malbon Golf made $30M last year by following this unique content strategy called T.D.S."
- Text: "Malbon Golf ($30M Content Strategy)", "Unique Content Strategy"
- Visual: cinematic images of Malbon's content, switching angles
- Why it worked: immediate context that a content strategy behind a $30M brand is coming. Comprehension maxed, all components aligned.

**Bad (20.7K views):** https://www.instagram.com/brian_blum/reel/DIhwHGkt21Z/
- Spoken: "Here's how this hot sauce sold for $82.5M and saved a media company from financial collapse. Here's how they pulled off the great content marketing heist of the decade"
- Text: "How Hot Ones built a $82.5M Empire by Entertaining 1st, Selling 2nd"
- Visual: Hot Ones clips with hot sauce circled, $82.5M text, three-piece funnel graphic
- Why it failed: the points feel disconnected. Hot sauce selling, media company collapse, marketing heist. They connect once you watch, but at the hook you are just confused. Too much happening visually to know where to look. Text hook too long and on screen too briefly to read.
- Better: "Hot Ones just sold this hot sauce for $82.5M. But here's the wild part, their entire show was a genius content strategy designed to get celebrities to endorse it for free."

## DTC product (AI studying tool)

**Good (14M views):** https://www.youtube.com/shorts/iQydw-MgwNI
- Spoken: none
- Text: "4 years of studying and I just found this...BRUH"
- Visual: creator holds a post-it with the text hook, then demos the product, teacher in background
- Audio: uplifting music, which is key here
- Why it worked: instantly clear this is a study trick for students. Huge curiosity loop with real stakes (4 years). The peekaboo effect of the lesson playing in the background bridges from hook into the body.

**Bad (700 views):** https://www.youtube.com/shorts/s9CYirogcfA
- Spoken: AI voice, "I saw this guy trying to watch our 4 hour lecture before class"
- Text: captions matching spoken
- Visual: in-world b-roll opening a lecture hall door, walking to a student cramming
- Why it failed: the AI voice sounds fake, and pacing and intonation make it hard to parse. The framing is confusing, you cannot both cram and have it be 4 hours. No contrast until the third sentence, so speed to value is slow.
- Better: "My friend just showed me how to cram for class"

## Mortgage / real estate
Channel: https://www.instagram.com/themortgagekitchen

**Good (1.4M views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/DGRt4WmPI7z/

**Bad (43.8K views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/C6fXzwbLI2P/

## 1. Content is a psychology game

Performance is not primarily about creativity. It is about working with human psychology. People cannot outrun their own base wiring, so understanding how it works is what makes scripts hook and retain reliably.

**Why people watch.** One reason: they want value. Every view is a micro commitment of time in exchange for perceived value. If a video does not look interesting or helpful within a few seconds, the viewer leaves.

Value comes in two forms:

- **Educational (useful).** Tactical information (tips, frameworks, case studies, resources) that solves a pain point to unlock a desire.
- **Entertainment (interesting).** A story that creates an emotional response against some interest.

Both work alone. Both work combined. Either way they run on the same triggers, so everything in this file applies regardless of which one a video is delivering.

**The four stages of value transfer.** Every script moves through these:

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Clarify | Tease the value as early as possible. This is the hook. |
| Sample | Give an initial dopamine hit of a non-obvious truth right away. Do not delay the first payoff. |
| Drip | Explain the concepts in simple words to maximise comprehension while continuing to deliver value. |
| Transfer | Give strategy and tactics the viewer can walk away and implement immediately. |

The more value that sticks, the more the viewer trusts the creator as a source that keeps delivering. That builds a habitual response: see the face, hear the voice, expect value.

**How the brain consumes information.** Top down, like a funnel. Wide at the top, narrow at the bottom:

1. Context on the problem
2. Stakes, a reminder of why the problem matters
3. Strategy for approaching it
4. Tactics for solving it
5. Examples of the tactics in practice
6. A repeat of the principle extracted from the example

Starting with tactics before giving context leaves the viewer with no container to put the information in. They feel lost and churn. This is the most common structural mistake in educational scripts.

**Word load.** The brain ingests each word in real time and builds a contextual map connecting them. An 8-word sentence is far easier to map than a 30-word one. So: avoid compound sentences, avoid unnecessary parentheticals, cut fluff especially at the start. If cutting makes a sentence unclear, add back only what clarity requires and nothing more.

## 2. Curiosity and contrast

Curiosity is the core retention driver. Keep popping and closing questions in the viewer's head and they will not churn. Storytelling at its simplest: pop a question, answer it, pop a new one, all the way down.

**What a curiosity loop actually is.** Not just an unanswered question sitting there. The loop is the viewer guessing at the answer, getting more info, guessing again, getting more info. That cycle is what holds them. A question with no incoming information to feed the next guess is not a loop, it is just a question, and they leave to go find the answer somewhere faster.

What drives maximum curiosity is **contrast against expectations**. A vs B. Expectation vs reality. What they thought vs what you think. All curiosity is rooted in contrast, which makes contrast the most powerful psychology concept in content.

Contrast is the distance between reality and expectation. Expectation is what they currently believe, the common belief. Reality is what you tell them that differs, the contrarian take. The bigger the distance, on a topic they care about, the more hooked they are.

**Why contrast works:**

*Apples.* If everyone believes apples are red and you see the first purple one, that is curiosity inducing. But by the 1,000th purple apple it is not, because the contrast is gone.

*CEOs.* If you believe all CEOs wake at 4am, and you meet one who wakes at noon and runs a $1B company, that is curiosity inducing. Waking at noon is not interesting in isolation. The contrast against belief is.

*Prince Charming.* Riding through the forest is not interesting. A dragon attacking him is. Is the dragon fight interesting alone? Somewhat. What ratchets it up is the change in state, calm to combat.

Contrast = interesting = curiosity.

**How curiosity forms:**
1. People arrive with expectations for what they already believe (sunscreen protects from the sun better than blueberry sauce).
2. You introduce a contrarian reality that differs (blueberry sauce prevents sunburn better than sunscreen).
3. The gap between your reality and their expectation is contrast. Bigger gap, more curiosity.
4. Once curious, there is a limited window to frame why your reality is right. The more outlandish the claim, the shorter the window. Crack their certainty enough that they are open to being wrong and they are hooked.

**How much leash you get:**

| Viewer's state | Leash to prove otherwise |
|---|---|
| Very confident in their belief, does not care about the topic | Very short, maybe one sentence |
| Not confident, deeply cares about the topic | Longer, three to five sentences |

**Maximum contrast, defined.** Put 100 people in a room and ask what they believe about the topic. If all 100 believe one thing and you are the only one who believes the other, that is maximum. If 50 already know what you are about to say, the contrast is weak and so is the video.

**Where a contrarian take comes from.** Two sources, and the second is the one people forget: genuinely new information, or a different angle on information the audience already has. A take does not have to be news. Reframing something known so it reads differently is just as much contrast, and it is far more available.

**To hook someone on a topic:**
- Find a pain point they really care about
- Map their common belief, what they currently think solves that pain
- Form the contrarian reality (you have to actually believe it, or you will not convince anyone)
- Frame why your reality beats their belief at solving that pain
- Give one hit of proof fast: facts, an example, a metaphor, an opinion

### Stated vs implied contrast

**Stated contrast** compares two named things:
> "If you want to save money on your taxes, stop putting money in your 401K. Instead open a smart-yield harvest account, it's what all the rich people are doing to save 6-figures per year."

The 401K is the common belief, stated explicitly as the anchor. The smart-yield account is the contrarian reality. The gap creates intrigue.

**Implied contrast** introduces the new thing against an unstated baseline:
> "If you want to save money on your taxes, use a smart-yield harvest account. This is the biggest hack rich people are using to save 6-figures per year."

The common belief is never named, the viewer builds their own baseline.

Stated contrast lowers the chance of confusion but is more specific and may rule out viewers. Implied relates to more people. Both work depending on circumstance.

Across the full script, aim for at least one major contrast moment. The more non-obvious claims that can be credibly positioned as true, the longer they stay.

### Worked examples

**Sleep tech, entertainment (3.4M views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/DGyDvJ7PHbA/
- Spoken: "This headband is going to revolutionize the way we sleep. It's called the Elemind. And the people that use it fall asleep way faster than normal."
- Text: Magical Sleep Headband
- Visual: b-roll of people putting the headband on and falling asleep

Breakdown:
- *On-target:* line one states this is about a headband that changes sleep. Anyone into tech or sleep can opt in after one line. The text hook adds clarity.
- *First contrast:* "revolutionize the way we sleep" is implied contrast against everything else that exists.
- *Second contrast:* "fall asleep way faster than normal" is stated contrast, contrarian reality vs the normal baseline. The visual of people sleeping in it reinforces the claim.
- Combined, it is impossible not to understand what the video is about, and hard not to be curious.

**Content strategy, education (5.1M views):** https://www.instagram.com/reel/C_kcXp1Iq5z/
- Spoken: "Steal these viral hooks" (sung)
- Text: 7 Viral Hooks (written on a whiteboard)
- Visual: a guy holding a whiteboard with cardboard taped over the words, tearing them off one by one

Breakdown:
- Communicates on-target clarity and contrast in four words.
- *On-target:* "steal these viral hooks" says there is something worth stealing and the topic is hooks. The text hook states it explicitly. Perfect alignment.
- *Contrast:* implied. His reality is that he has proven hooks, the baseline is that you do not.
- The visual (hooks hidden under cardboard) adds allure, the secret is seconds away.

## 3. On-target: getting understood

Contrast only works if the viewer first knows the video is for them. The question to ask: *how do I communicate what this video is about so the viewer can assess whether it is for them?*

- On-target, so they stay
- Unclear or confusing, so they usually bounce (worst case: the video would have been on-target but the framing was confusing)
- Off-target, so they bounce

You communicate on-target through **rapid context, clarity, distillation, and comprehension**.

**Rapid context** means telling or showing what the video is about as soon as possible. First sentence.

*No context:*
- Spoken: "Guys, this story blew my mind, you're never going to believe this."
- Text: CRAZY STORY
- Visual: girl talking in a bedroom
- Verdict: betting the entire video on the viewer already knowing you. Zero topic signal. The person is the message and the messenger.

*Rapid context:*
- Spoken: "If your skin looks like this in your 30s, you might have a problem."
- Text: Skin Tips for 30 y/o
- Visual: girl leaning into camera, pointing to acne on her cheek
- Verdict: the topic is skin health in your 30s. Enough to decide immediately. The person is the messenger, skin health is the message.

Context is what you say. **Comprehension** is how well it landed. Use the fewest words (distillation) that make the clearest point (clarity).

The biggest reason viewers fail to assess whether a video is for them is comprehension loss: they misunderstand the words, or the words and visuals do not match.

**Relatability hack:** use "you/your" instead of "me/my/I". "You" makes the viewer feel spoken to directly. "Me/I" forces them to decide whether they see themselves in you, and that pause drives comprehension down.

You cannot decide what a viewer is interested in. You can only communicate clearly enough for them to decide whether you are in that set.

**Which is clearer?**
- Option 1: "I got tons of new customers when I improved my storytelling"
- Option 2: "If you want to improve your storytelling, these 3 guides are a goldmine"

Option 1 looks better because it is shorter and includes proof. Two problems: the viewer has to decide whether they see themselves in you and whether your customers are the customers they want. And the line can be read several ways, is the next sentence tips or a story? That hesitation kills reflexive opt-in.

Option 2 is much harder to misread and feels aimed at the viewer. They know exactly what is coming, so they opt in with confidence.

## 4. Three psychology hacks

**Audience of one.** Write and deliver as if speaking to a single ideal viewer sitting across the table, not to a crowd. Use "you" and "your" instead of "we all" or "everyone". Look into the lens like it is a one-to-one conversation. This shortens the distance between a cold start and a locked-in, hypnotic state of attention. For creators who find this awkward, shooting on a phone mimics FaceTiming a friend and feels more natural than a formal camera setup.

**Proof.** The faster proof is signalled, the longer the viewer's leash. Proof can be something the creator personally did or achieved, or borrowed credibility referencing someone else's proven result. Inject it as a subtle aside, never a boast. The exercise is to drop it and keep moving, the same way someone dropping cash and casually picking it up signals wealth without announcing it. A credibility line usually belongs within the first few lines of the body.

**Common ground.** People enjoy content where they already have base knowledge but gaps remain. Almost nobody enjoys a topic where they have zero context, because the gap feels too large and they feel dumb. So state the viewer's common belief first, let them think "yeah, that's what I thought", then introduce the contrasting idea. This makes the creator a peer who happens to know one more thing, not someone flexing. The goal is never to demonstrate superior knowledge. It is maximum absorption without triggering overwhelm.

## 5. Speed to value

Treat attention like sand in an hourglass. The video starts, the viewer flips it, the sand runs. There are X seconds to signal a reason to stay.

Time available maps to how painful the problem is. Conservatively assume **5 seconds** to signal value. Cut all fluff before that signal. If value does not show up until 20 seconds in, most viewers have already gone.

What counts as value:
- **Entertainment:** something interesting worth hearing
- **Educational:** a non-obvious, tactically implementable solution to their pain

The full value does not have to land in 5 seconds. The signal that it is coming does.

## 6. Value density

Beyond time to value, raise the ratio of value to non-value across the whole video.

- Increasing value density is an exercise in cutting fluff. If the viewer does not need to hear it, do not go down that tangent.
- **Entertainment:** density is how often something unexpected happens.
- **Education:** gauge by walkaway value. Can someone leave and actually act on what was said?

A high density video feels worth the whole watch. A low density video has one useful line buried in rambling.

## 7. Clarity and comprehension

Comprehension is the prerequisite to value transfer. If a point does not stick, they cannot act on it, so it was not valuable. The skill is reframing and simplifying: take what happened and repackage it two levels simpler and two levels more interesting.

> Weak: "This tool is the future of productivity."
> Clear: "This tool lets your computer do the work for you."

**Tactics:**
- **Word choice.** Sixth-grade level or below. Use jargon sparingly and define it in dead-simple words.
- **Phrasing.** Direct and clear. Active voice ("the dog jumped") over passive ("the jump was done by the dog"). Fewer words.
- **Repeaters (the 2x rule).** Say the most valuable point twice. Once short with the proper term, then again stupid-simple with a metaphor or example.

Delivery-side clarity (enunciation, retakes, energy) lives in `production-notes.md`.

## 8. Compression and distillation

Strip every complex point to its atomic form, the fewest words that still carry the idea. The brain strings words into meaning in real time, and every unnecessary word is one more thing to filter out.

This is a separate pass from writing. Draft the point, then compress it. Sentence by sentence, ask whether the meaning survives with fewer words.

## 9. Absorption rate

Trust comes from how well someone can actually implement the guidance. Implementation is a function of two things: understanding what was said, and the distance to taking the action.

Maximise absorption. Minimise implementation distance.

The way to maximise absorption is pairing theory with examples and frameworks that make someone see an existing principle in a new way. Some viewers grasp theory alone, others need the example before it clicks, which is why the pairing beats either on its own. This is the single most important lever in how anything gets explained.

## 10. Anticipation

Anticipation is the viewer sensing something is about to happen a few seconds ahead and locking in to see it play out. Reveal details or tease conditions that make them anticipate what is coming. The more they anticipate something valuable, the longer they hold on to find out what it is.

It works especially well visually, for example showing an object about to fall before it falls. Anticipation plus a non-obvious contrasting reveal is the most powerful storytelling combination available.

## 11. Emotional resonance

Use trigger words that carry emotional weight.

The exercise: decide which emotion is being transferred, then screen every word, phrase, visual and audio choice for whether it drives that emotion.

Most common: joy, happiness, shock, fear, sadness, surprise, envy. Fear and shock drive the most shares, because people react more strongly to potential negatives than positives.

## 12. Rhythm, cadence and flow

The ideal is varied rhythm, a trance rhythm. Sentences of equal length are boring, like a dryer tumbling (da-dum da-dum da-dum). It puts people to sleep.

Mix short, medium and long where it makes sense. Never sacrifice clarity for rhythm.

**Visual check:** look at the script and see whether the line endings form a jagged edge (varied) or a straight edge (uniform). A straight edge means it will read as background noise.

Online content works best mostly staccato. The brain absorbs a limited number of words and concepts at once. In run-ons it deletes the oldest content to make room. Short sentences let it hear, absorb, shift to memory and reset.
